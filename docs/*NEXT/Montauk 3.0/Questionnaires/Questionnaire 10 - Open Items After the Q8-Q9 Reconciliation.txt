A1. Does the Montauk 2.0 daily signal keep printing?

Context. On 2026-08-02 you said: "im the only one who can say 2.0 is done. which
it is. thats on pause/retired etc." That is recorded in D104 as your sole
authority, exercised. What it does not settle is whether "retired" means
development stopped, or the daily signal stopped too.

Plain version. Montauk 3.0 does not exist yet. Phase 1 has not started. The
earliest a 3.0 strategy could earn Gold, complete its cooling period, and be
activated is months away.

  - If "retired" means DEVELOPMENT is done, 2.0 keeps emitting risk_on/risk_off
    every day, frozen, until a 3.0 strategy replaces it. You are covered
    throughout.

  - If "retired" means the SIGNAL stops now, you have no trusted daily signal at
    all until 3.0 earns its first Gold row. That is the exact no-signal gap
    Questionnaire 9 item 4 was written to prevent.

Both are legitimate. You may simply not want to follow a 2.0 signal you no longer
trust, and that is your call to make - but it should be made deliberately rather
than inherited from an ambiguous word.

Question. Does 2.0 keep producing the daily signal until you activate a 3.0
strategy, or does it stop now?

Suggested answer. Keep it printing, frozen and clearly labelled "legacy 2.0
signal - no further development." No new strategies, no tuning, no
recertification, no leaderboard changes. It simply keeps emitting the signal from
the strategy that is Active today, so you are never without one. It retires the
day you approve a 3.0 strategy as Active, in the same action, exactly as D104
describes.

Reasoning. Freezing development and freezing the signal are separable, and the
cost of separating them is nearly zero: a frozen 2.0 needs no attention. The cost
of NOT separating them is months with no certified daily instruction. If you have
decided you no longer trust the 2.0 signal enough to follow it, say so here and
the answer changes - but then that distrust should be recorded as its own reason,
not as a side effect of retiring the codebase.

Your answer:
Keep printing, frozen. Suggested answer accepted.



A2. What happens to the Montauk 2.0 code and its Gold artifacts?

Context. D101 already decided that the strategies currently holding 2.0 Gold are
the first corpus the 3.0 pipeline tests, with no grandfathering - they must earn
3.0 Gold on their own. What was never asked is what happens to the 2.0 SYSTEM: the
Python engine, the leaderboard, the viz surfaces, the spike runs.

Plain version. Three things could happen to it, and they are not mutually
exclusive:

  (a) It stays runnable in place as the legacy signal source and reference, until
      3.0 replaces it. Required if A1 keeps the signal printing.
  (b) Its Gold artifacts, trade ledgers, and leaderboard are preserved as
      migration evidence and as fixtures the 3.0 engine must reproduce during
      parity testing.
  (c) The code itself is archived rather than deleted, so a 3.0 defect can always
      be diagnosed against a working reference implementation.

Question. Confirm what is preserved and what, if anything, is removed.

Suggested answer. All three. Keep 2.0 runnable in place, preserve every Gold
artifact and trade ledger as parity fixtures and migration evidence, and archive
rather than delete the code. Nothing is removed until after 3.0 cutover, and even
then the archive stays. Storage on the appliance is 120 GB and the 2.0 footprint
is trivial against that; there is no reason to reclaim it and a real reason not
to - a second implementation is the only independent check on a 3.0 engine bug.

Reasoning. The most dangerous moment for 3.0 is the first time it disagrees with
2.0 on a trade. Having 2.0 runnable turns that from an argument into a
measurement.

Your answer:
Preserve all of it. Suggested answer accepted. Added: "and then i think its
in there but i want to test the montauk 2.0 strategies first to see if they are
still good with the updated data pipeline and validation system"



A3. How broad should the starting Rust building-block library be?

Context. You settled D96 with the Lego principle: the agent builds anything it
wants from existing blocks, and needs your approval before creating a new one.
That makes the size of the initial block set the single biggest driver of how
often you get interrupted.

The Rust policy document currently says the first primitive registry is
"intentionally small and fixture-heavy." That was written before D96, when the
escape hatch meant a missing primitive was not a hard stop. It now pulls directly
against the Lego principle: a small registry means a kid with six Lego pieces.

Plain version. Every gap in the library becomes an approval request sitting in
your Slack waiting on you. While it waits, the search keeps running on the old
vocabulary. If you are busy for three weeks, the machine explores a frozen idea
space for three weeks.

Granularity matters more than raw count. Fine-grained blocks (EMA, ATR,
crossover, comparison, lookback, boolean AND/OR, position state) recombine into an
enormous space. Coarse blocks (whole strategy templates) barely compose at all,
and nearly every new idea needs a new template.

Question. Should the initial registry be deliberately over-provisioned with
fine-grained composable primitives, accepting more up-front build work, rather
than starting minimal?

Suggested answer. Over-provision, and build the registry fine-grained. Writing 60
primitives once while you are already in build mode is far cheaper than writing 5
more every few weeks in response to proposals, and it is what makes the Lego
principle real rather than nominal. Correctness is not sacrificed: every primitive
still ships with fixtures and tests, which is what "fixture-heavy" was protecting
and that part should stay.

Reasoning. The whole cost of D96 is measured in how often you become the
bottleneck. Registry breadth is the one lever that reduces it, and it is cheapest
to pull at the start.

Your answer:
Over-provision, fine-grained. Suggested answer accepted.



A4. When does the 90-day empty-board clock start?

Context. D102 says: run the pipeline as specified for 90 days, and if nothing has
earned Gold in that window, review whether the bar is correctly high or a gate is
mis-implemented. The start date was never specified.

Plain version. The clock could start when the validator is first built, when the
full conveyor first runs end to end, or when Phase 1's measured contract is signed
and Gold certification is actually enabled. These are potentially months apart. A
clock that starts too early will expire while the system is still half-built and
trigger a review of nothing.

Question. When does the 90-day window begin?

Suggested answer. On the first day Gold certification is ENABLED - that is, after
you approve the measured Phase 1 package and the signed validation contract is in
force. Before that moment the pipeline is not capable of producing Gold, so an
empty board carries no information.

Reasoning. The review is meant to distinguish "correctly strict" from "broken."
Neither is diagnosable while the exam itself is still being calibrated.

Your answer:
When Gold certification is enabled. Suggested answer accepted.



A5. Does the 5:00 PM Pacific signal deadline apply during 3.0 shadow operation?

Context. D87 fixed the daily signal deadline at 5:00 PM Pacific with a hard alert
at 6:00 PM. D104 has 3.0 running in shadow alongside the legacy 2.0 signal for
some period before cutover.

Plain version. During shadow, 3.0 is producing a recommendation you are not
following. If the deadline and hard alert apply to shadow output, a 3.0 hiccup
wakes you up about a signal you were not going to act on. If they do not apply,
you also will not find out that 3.0 misses its deadline until the day it becomes
authoritative - which is the worst possible day to discover it.

Question. Does the hard alert fire for shadow signals, or only for the
authoritative one?

Suggested answer. Track and record the deadline during shadow, but alert at a
lower severity - it appears in the daily digest as a missed shadow deadline rather
than firing the hard 6:00 PM alarm. The hard alert attaches to whichever signal is
currently authoritative: 2.0's today, 3.0's after cutover. Shadow deadline misses
are commissioning evidence and must be clean before you approve cutover.

Reasoning. You get the information without the false alarms, and a persistent
shadow-deadline problem blocks cutover under D104 condition 4 rather than
surfacing after it.

Your answer:
Track it, alert softly. Suggested answer accepted.



A6. Where do block proposals and approval requests arrive?

Context. On D96 you said the block-proposal surface would be "another slack
channel or similar." Under D107 there are also seven standing approval types that
need to reach you (Phase 1 package, third score pillar or new veto, block
proposals, restore drills, Active switches, completion declaration, Chimera
scheduling).

Plain version. Either these get their own Slack channels, or they arrive in one
channel as differently-shaped cards. The daily digest and alerts already have to
live somewhere too.

Question. Separate channels per surface, or one channel with typed cards?

Suggested answer. Two channels. One operational channel carries the daily digest,
alerts, and status - the things you read. One approvals channel carries every item
that is waiting on a decision from you - the things you act on. Everything in the
second channel is by definition blocking something, so its unread count is a real
work queue rather than noise.

Reasoning. Mixing a daily digest you skim with approvals that block the machine
means either the digest trains you to ignore the channel, or the approvals make
the digest feel urgent. Splitting by "read" versus "act" is the split that matches
how you will actually use it.

Your answer:
Two channels: read vs act. Suggested answer accepted.
